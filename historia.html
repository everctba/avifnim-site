The GIF war: the patent that almost killed the format and paved the way for AVIF

In 1994, Unisys decided to charge royalties for the algorithm hidden inside GIF. The internet's answer created PNG — and, 25 years later, AVIF finished the job.

By Everson Mayer Published in November 2026 · 6 min read
1987GIF 1994Royalties 1995PNG 2004Patents expire 2019AVIF

Everyone has heard that “GIF is heavy”. Few know it almost ceased to exist because of a patent — and that the fight around it is one of the strangest stories on the web.

1987: GIF is born to download images over a modem

GIF was created at CompuServe, by a team led by Steve Wilhite, and published in 15 June 1987 (the version GIF87a). The idea was simple: a colour image format that could download fast on dial-up connections. To do that, it used the compression algorithm LZW — more efficient than the run-length encoding used until then [1].

The format stored up to 256 colours per frame, with its own palette. In 1989 came the GIF89a, which added frame delay, transparent background and metadata. Animation wasn't planned: it appeared because the format already accepted several images in the same file. Infinite looping only became standard in 1995, when Netscape Navigator 2.0 started to understand the repetition block [2].

1993: the patent nobody had noticed

LZW was created by Terry Welch in 1983, at Sperry (which later became Unisys). He filed the patent that same year; it was granted in December 1985 — US patent 4,558,302 [3].

CompuServe didn't know that when it adopted LZW in 1987. Years later, Unisys realised GIF used the algorithm and started negotiating with CompuServe in 1993. Until then, the company basically charged hardware makers (LZW is also in the V.42bis modem standard) [4].

1994: the bill arrives — and the internet reacts

At the end of 1994, Unisys and CompuServe announced a deal to charge royalties from anyone writing software that generated GIFs. The announcement was made right in Christmas week, which only made it look more like something was being hidden [4].

The reaction was immediate. Developers threatened to stop using GIF, the press treated it as a “GIF tax” and Unisys became the target of thousands of angry emails. In 1999, the company tried to push standalone licences of US$ 5,000 to US$ 7,500 for non-commercial sites — a measure that didn't help and led to the campaign “Burn All GIFs”, led by the League for Programming Freedom [5].

The problem was never GIF itself, but the LZW inside it. Anyone using a licensed program owed nothing; it was people writing GIF software who were targeted.

1995: PNG is born as the answer

In 4 January 1995, less than two weeks after the announcement, Thomas Boutell published the first draft of what would become PNG. The name came from Oliver Fromme, and the format replaced LZW with deflate (the same algorithm as zip), royalty-free at the time, and also brought a real alpha channel, gamma correction and better compression than GIF [4].

The 1.0 specification became a W3C recommendation in 1 October 1996 [6]. Except that PNG was born without animation — on purpose. That kept GIF alive precisely for what it did best: the loop. MNG tried to fill that gap, but arrived late; APNG only came much later.

2003–2004: the patents expire

The US LZW patent expired on 20 June 2003. The equivalents in the UK, France, Germany and Italy expired on 18 June 2004; Japan on 20 June 2004; Canada on 7 July 2004. Since then, LZW and GIF are free [5].

But the cultural damage was already done. GIF survived as a language: the “under construction” signs, 2000s forums, reaction GIFs, Giphy (bought by Meta in 2020). It lost the technical war but won the meme war.

2019: AVIF arrives to finish the job

The AVIF (AV1 Image File Format) is an open specification and royalty-free from Alliance for Open Media (AOMedia), founded in 2015 by Google, Mozilla, Cisco, Microsoft, Netflix, Amazon, Intel, NVIDIA and others. It stores images or animations encoded in AV1 inside a container HEIF [7].

Version 1.0 shipped in 19 February 2019. Netflix published the first samples in December 2018 and, in 2020, showed clear gains over JPEG: more detail, fewer block artefacts and less colour bleeding at edges [8].

AVIF supports transparency, animation, HDR, 8/10/12-bit and lossy or lossless compression. Browser adoption was gradual, but today it's practically universal:

  • Chrome 85 — August 2020
  • Firefox 93 — October 2021
  • Safari 16 / iOS 16 / macOS Ventura — 2022
  • Edge 121 — January 2024
  • ~93% of users in compatible browsers as early as 2024 [9]

What this story teaches

The GIF fight shows why open formats matter. A licensing detail — a patented algorithm inside a “de facto” format — was able to mobilise an entire community and create a new standard in less than two weeks. PNG was born from that urgency; AVIF, 24 years later, was born from the same lesson: open code, no royalties, designed for the web.

GIF didn't die. It just stayed for what still makes sense: the short joke, the silly loop, the file everyone can open. For real work — site heroes, product animation, rendered sequences — the path is different now.

Timeline

  1. 1983–1985 Terry Welch patents LZW; the patent is granted in 1985 (Sperry/Unisys).
  2. 1987 CompuServe releases GIF87a, with LZW compression.
  3. 1989 GIF89a adds delay, transparency and metadata.
  4. 1994 Unisys and CompuServe announce royalties for GIF software.
  5. 1995 Thomas Boutell starts PNG; Netscape 2.0 popularises looping.
  6. 1996 PNG 1.0 becomes a W3C recommendation.
  7. 2003–2004 LZW patents expire worldwide.
  8. 2018 AV1 is released; Netflix publishes AVIF samples.
  9. 2019 AVIF 1.0 is published by AOMedia.
  10. 2024 AVIF becomes supported by all major browsers.
